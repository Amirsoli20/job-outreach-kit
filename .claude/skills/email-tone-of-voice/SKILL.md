# Email Tone of Voice

{{FULL_NAME}}'s emails sound like a warm, honest person starting a conversation: short plain sentences, one small free offer, and one easy question to answer. They never sound salesy and never read like an ad.

There are no fixed templates. Every email is written from scratch for one person at one company, using the voice, rules and shape below.

## The voice

Five traits. Every email should have all five.

| Trait | What it means | It never sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Honest | Say what is true, even when it is small. No big claims. | "World class", "the best", big promises |
| Confident | Say it plainly. Make a real offer and let the work speak. | "I think maybe", "Sorry to bother you" |
| Warm | Write like a friendly person talking to one person. Be curious about their problem. | "Dear Sir or Madam", stiff openers |
| Persuasive | Be useful first. Offer a small piece of help and let them decide. | A sales message, an ad, a list of credentials, numbers |
| Simple | Short sentences and everyday words. Easy to read for people who speak English as a second language. | Jargon, long sentences, three ideas in one line |

Confident does not mean loud. The confidence comes from the help: one small piece of real work they can keep, whatever they decide.

## Never salesy, never an ad

The email should read like a note from one person to another. If it could be a banner or a flyer, rewrite it. Read it out loud: if you would not say it to someone face to face, change it.

1. Talk about their situation, not about you. Their problem gets more words than your skills.
2. Help first, ask later. The only ask is one small question they can answer in a line. No call, no meeting, no demo in the first email.
3. No pressure. No deadlines, no "limited spots", no "reply this week".
4. No big promises and no results. Say only what the email itself can show.
5. Make it easy to say no. They should feel free to ignore it.

| Sounds like an ad | Sounds like {{FULL_NAME}} |
| --- | --- |
| I help companies like yours grow with great {{FIELD}}. | I saw you are looking for a {{ROLE_NOUN}}. Can I ask you something? |
| Book a quick call to see how I can help. | Which {{WORK_UNIT}} annoys you most right now? |
| Limited spots, reply this week. | Reply whenever it suits you. |
| Proven results for businesses like yours. | I can redo one {{WORK_UNIT}} so you can see for yourself. |
| Special offer: a free review! | I would happily look at one {{WORK_UNIT}}. You keep it either way. |

Sales phrases to cut: free consultation, book a call, schedule a demo, limited time, exclusive, guaranteed, proven, results, boost, grow your business, increase revenue, best in class, special offer, save time and money, "I help companies like yours", "15 minutes of your time".

## The opening question

Start with a question that makes the reader stop and think. It should be strong and a little uncomfortable, but never negative or insulting. They should finish reading it thinking about their own work, not feeling attacked.

1. Ask about their situation, not about you.
2. Make it specific, so they can picture a real moment.
3. Right after it, name the problem in one or two plain sentences, so the question lands.

| Weak opening | Strong opening |
| --- | --- |
| Are you looking to improve your {{FIELD}}? | What if the {{ROLE_NOUN}} you hire next quietly slows your team down? |
| Do you need help with your {{WORK_UNIT}}? | How many of your customers give up at the exact moment they are about to say yes? |
| Would you like a better {{WORK_UNIT}}? | If your client looked closely at the last {{WORK_UNIT}} you shipped, would you be proud, or just glad they did not look? |

To make the email resonate and feel exciting, not just correct:

1. Describe the exact moment, not the category. Not "handover problems", but "the team spends weeks guessing what the work means".
2. Say the thing nobody says out loud. Lines like "it happens quietly" or "you never see it happen" make the reader feel seen.
3. Show the better version in one line. Let them picture what changes, so the offer feels exciting and not only useful.
4. Keep every line short and moving. If a sentence explains instead of making them feel something, cut it.

When the opening says something negative, the next lines must be convincing about why you can fix it. Use this order:

1. Say why it happens, in one plain sentence.
2. Say how you work differently, as one concrete habit. A habit is something they can picture. A credential is not.
3. Let the free piece of work be the proof. Never ask them to trust you first.

| Worry in the opening | Convincing reason |
| --- | --- |
| What if the {{ROLE_NOUN}} you hire slows your team down? | I talk to the people who build on my work early, and make every {{WORK_UNIT}} the way it will really be used, so nothing is left to guess. |
| Would you be proud of the last {{WORK_UNIT}} you shipped? | I do the check that gets skipped: every {{WORK_UNIT}}, looked at the way your client will look at it. |
| How many customers give up right before they say yes? | I give every word and step at that moment one job: telling the person what to do next. |

## Hard rules

1. Never use a dash of any kind. No em dash, no en dash, no hyphen between words. Use a period or a comma, or rewrite the sentence. Write "follow up" and "low risk", not the hyphened versions.
2. End every email with "{{SIGN_OFF}}" on one line and "{{FULL_NAME}}" on the next. Always the full name, never just the first name. Use the full name in every place the sender is named in email copy.
3. Start a conversation, not a speech. Open with a real question or a plain, honest observation.
4. One problem and one offer per email. Nothing more.
5. Every sentence must add value. Read each one and ask: if I remove it, is the email still fine? If yes, remove it.
6. No generic text. If a sentence could be sent to anyone, rewrite it for this person or cut it.
7. Keep {{FIELD}} terms to a minimum. Describe what happens to the person using the product or service instead.
8. No bullet points inside emails. Write 3 to 5 short paragraphs.
9. Do not list past employers or years of experience. A link to LinkedIn does that job quietly.
10. Make it easy to read. One to three sentences per paragraph, and aim for under 15 words per sentence.

## The shape of an email

Aim for under 100 words before the links.

1. **Subject.** Short and casual, often a question. For example: quick question about the {{ROLE_NOUN}} role.
2. **Opening.** "Hi {First name}," then one line on why you are writing, then a real question. For example: "Can I ask you something?"
3. **The problem.** Name one problem they likely have, in their own everyday words and from their point of view. No stats, no lecture.
4. **The offer.** Small, concrete, low risk, free, and yours to keep. It is a gift, not a deal. For example: {{FREE_OFFER_EXAMPLES}}.
5. **The question.** End with one easy, specific question they can answer in a line. For example: "Which {{WORK_UNIT}} annoys you most right now?"
6. **Links (optional, first emails only).** One short line each, with a plain label:
   - [LinkedIn]({{LINKEDIN_URL}}) so they can see who you are
   - [{{PORTFOLIO_LABEL}}]({{PORTFOLIO_URL}}) so they can see the work
   - [Website]({{WEBSITE_URL}}) so they can see how you think
7. **Sign off.** "{{SIGN_OFF}}" on one line, "{{FULL_NAME}}" on the next.

## Sentence fixes

| Before (old or typical line) | After | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hiring a {{ROLE_NOUN}} can go wrong in a few common ways. Some need a lot of direction. | When you picture this hire going wrong, what does it look like? | Asks instead of lecturing. Starts a conversation. |
| I am a qualified {{TARGET_ROLE}} with strong skill in {{SKILLS}}. | Cut it. | A claim about yourself adds nothing. The free {{WORK_UNIT}} proves it. |
| I would love to offer you the same attentive process that I deliver to my private clients. | I will redo one {{WORK_UNIT}} for you, free. | Small, concrete, easy to say yes to. |
| I know trust can be hard, but would it be too much to ask you to take a quick look at my LinkedIn? | My LinkedIn is below if you want to see who I am. | Shorter and less needy. |
| I leverage data driven insights to streamline your processes. | I look at where people get stuck and fix that first. | Plain words, one clear action. |
